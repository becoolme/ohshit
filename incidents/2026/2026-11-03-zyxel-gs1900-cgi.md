An unauthenticated attacker who can reach the affected switch’s management CGI can corrupt request parsing and execute operating-system commands. Zyxel describes a LAN-based attacker; Binarly argues that the HTTP path has no protocol-level adjacency requirement. Keep both assessments visible: the vendor’s CVSS 3.1 score is **8.8**, while Binarly’s is **9.8**, and neither score is a victim count.

Binarly recovered the exploit from infrastructure associated with the JITTERLY/SIXZUT campaign and tested it against the ten firmware releases previously advertised as fixes for CVE-2026-7273. The earlier counter check ran after an attacker-controlled copy; the exploit still worked. Zyxel treats the newly reported missing size check as a separate flaw, **CVE-2026-107441**. Patch 3 constrains the copy and validates the counter first. All ten model-specific fixed builds are listed above; a firmware filename for another GS1900 model is not interchangeable.

The researcher links the captured exploit to GreyNoise’s earlier campaign evidence involving **996 switches across 48 countries**. That historical campaign was initially attributed to CVE-2026-7273. It is not a separately verified census of CVE-2026-107441 victims, nor proof that every listed malware sample was installed on a switch. Binarly’s findings and the Canadian Cyber Centre’s later warning support urgent treatment of real exploitation; the CNA’s earlier CISA assessment still records none. The new identifier must not inherit the older identifier’s KEV entry or federal deadline.

L4 reflects the researcher’s evidence of an in-use pre-authentication exploit and an ineffective earlier fix. Restrict access, apply the correct new firmware and investigate potential prior compromise. The sources do not publish a universal recovery procedure or prove that installing firmware removes every implant; remediation of a compromised deployment may also require trusted recovery and credential replacement with support.
